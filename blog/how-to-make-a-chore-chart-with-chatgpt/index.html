How to Make a Chore Chart with ChatGPT (That Lasts Past Wednesday)

By Traci Anderson · November 23, 2026

My ADHD brain loves making a chore chart. Color-coded, little checkboxes, maybe a sticker system. Keeping it going is a different story. By Wednesday the chart is still on the fridge, nobody is looking at it, and the dishwasher is somehow everyone's least favorite appliance again.

The hard part isn't the pretty chart. It's the boring thinking first: what actually needs doing, who can do what, and whether the split is fair enough that nobody stages a protest. An AI helper is good at that first draft. It can sort a messy list, balance the minutes, and lay it out as a table you can print.

It doesn't know your kids, your house, or who "forgets" to feed the dog. You do. So the bot drafts, and the grown-up decides.

These prompts work in ChatGPT or any similar chatbot. Fill in the brackets, paste, and edit what you get.

Set the house rules first

Paste this before anything else, so the helper knows its job and its limits.

Copy-paste prompt 1
You're my chore-chart helper. Draft only: I decide every chore, every assignment, and any rewards. I print and post the chart myself.

I'll use nicknames, not full names. Don't ask for last names, schools, addresses, or photos.

Keep it short and plain. No parenting lectures, no guilt, and no reward systems unless I ask for one. If something is missing, ask me one question instead of guessing.

Nicknames are plenty. "Bug," "Kid 2," and "Captain Dishes" all work, and the bot doesn't need anything more.

Dump every chore, then let it sort

Don't tidy the list first. Just get it out of your head, including the stuff only you notice, like the lint trap and the mystery cup collection under the couch.

Copy-paste prompt 2
Here's everyone in the house and about how much time each person has on a school or work day:
[Nickname, age, about ___ minutes on weekdays, ___ on weekends]

Here's every chore I can think of, messy is fine:
[list]

Sort the chores into daily, a few times a week, weekly, and monthly. Give each one a rough time in minutes. Flag anything that sounds like two chores pretending to be one (like "clean kitchen"), and split it into smaller jobs.

That last line matters. "Clean your room" is a whole afternoon of negotiation. "Clothes in the hamper" is two minutes and a clear finish line.

Split it fairly by age and time

Fair doesn't mean everyone gets the same number of chores. It means the minutes make sense for each person.

Copy-paste prompt 3
Using the sorted list, draft a fair weekly split.

Rules:
- Match chores to each person's age and the minutes they have. I'll tell you if something is too much or too little.
- Balance the total minutes per week, not the number of chores.
- Give the least-liked chores (I'll mark them with a star) to a rotation, not one person forever.
- Leave the adults real jobs too, so the chart doesn't look like a kids-only punishment.

Show a table: person, chores, minutes per week. Then list anything you weren't sure about so I can decide.

Read the table with your own eyes. You know which kid can be trusted with a mop and which one is still learning what "wipe the counter" means. If the split feels off, say "Kid 2 is doing too much, move 20 minutes to me" and let it redo the math.

Turn it into a chart you can print

Once the split looks right, ask for the actual chart. Keep it simple. A chart covered in fifteen columns gets ignored faster.

Copy-paste prompt 4
Turn the weekly split into a printable chore chart.

Make a table with the days of the week across the top and each person's chores down the side, with an empty box to check off. Put each person's chores together. Use short words a [age]-year-old can read.

Keep it to one page. No clip art descriptions, no rewards column unless I ask. Then give me a plain-text version I can paste into a document or a notes app.

Paste the table into a doc or spreadsheet, bump up the font, and print it. Or copy it onto a whiteboard if your family is more of a marker crowd.

Add tiny morning and bedtime lists

Some chores are really part of a routine. These short lists often help more than the big chart.

Copy-paste prompt 5
From the chore chart, pull out anything that belongs in a morning or bedtime routine for each person.

Make two short checklists per person: morning (max 5 steps) and bedtime (max 5 steps). Put them in the order they'd actually happen. Use simple words, one action per line. Don't add steps I didn't mention.

Five steps is the point. A bedtime list with twelve items is a novel, and nobody reads novels at 8:45 p.m.

Do a two-minute Sunday tweak

Here's where my charts usually die. Things change, the chart doesn't, and everyone quietly stops using it. So instead of making a new chart, I patch the old one.

Copy-paste prompt 6
Here's last week's chore chart: [paste it].

What happened: [Kid 1 had practice three nights, the dog chore never got done, I did all the laundry again].

Adjust next week's chart. Move chores off the busy nights, rotate the starred chores, and fix anything that clearly didn't work. Show me only what changed, then the updated chart. Don't add new chores.

"Show me only what changed" is the ADHD-friendly part. You don't have to re-read the whole chart to see the two things that moved.

Safer setup

The helper drafts. You decide. That goes for every chore, every assignment, and every reward.

Review each chore for safety yourself before it goes on the chart. A bot doesn't know your kitchen, your cleaning products, or your kid, so you decide who's ready for the stove, sharp knives, cleaning sprays, ladders, or the lawn mower. If you're not sure, leave it off or keep it as a "with a grown-up" job.

Use nicknames, not full names. Don't paste photos of your kids, schools, addresses, or anything private into a chat. Nothing gets sent, bought, or shared without your OK.
