ADHD & planning

How to Use AI as a Daily Planner When You Have ADHD

By Traci Anderson · November 20, 2026

A lot of planners assume I will look at a long list and cheerfully begin at the top. That is not how my brain works. I can write a beautiful schedule at 8 a.m. and feel like I've already failed it by noon.

I'm a maker with ADHD. I don't use an AI helper as a coach, a therapist, or a parent. I use it as a calm whiteboard: I dump everything out, it helps me sort, and I pick one small next step. If the day changes, we redo the afternoon. No lecture.

These prompts are planning help, not medical advice, therapy, or treatment. They work in ChatGPT or any similar chatbot. Change the brackets to fit your day.

Set the tone first, so it doesn't pep-talk you

If you don't tell it how to talk to you, some chatbots get chipper. I don't need a hype speech. I need short lists and no guilt when a plan slips.

Copy-paste prompt 1
You're my daily planning helper. I have ADHD (or I think I do). Keep answers short, kind, and specific. No pep talks, no guilt, no "you should have."

Draft only: I send, buy, book, and schedule everything myself. Never ask for passwords or card numbers.

My usual best hours: [morning / afternoon / evening].
Fixed things I can't move: [work hours, pickups, appointments].
I often underestimate time, so leave buffers. If my list is too big for the day, help me move things without making it a moral issue.

If I say I'm stuck, give me one two-minute first step — not a full new plan — unless I ask for the plan.

Paste that at the start of a new chat and leave it there. You can reuse the same chat for a week if it still remembers your rules. If it forgets, paste the setup again.

Get it out of your head before you plan the day

The plan goes wrong when I try to organize and remember at the same time. First I dump. Then I sort.

Copy-paste prompt 2
Brain dump time. I'm going to paste everything in my head, messy is fine. Don't comment while I dump. When I say "done," sort it into:

- NOW: max 3 items, most important first
- LATER: a suggested day or "this week" is enough
- LET IT GO: a few words on why it can wait or be dropped
- NEEDS A DATE: anything that is actually an appointment or a bill, so I can put it on a calendar myself

Then give me the first tiny step for NOW item 1 — something I can do in two minutes. Don't add tasks I didn't mention.

Three things in NOW is the whole trick. A fourth item can be true and still belong in LATER. That's not laziness. That's a day that might actually happen.

Make a plan that assumes the day is shorter than it looks

Time blindness is real for me. I ask for a plan with only three must-dos, breaks, and a buffer, based on how much energy I actually have — not the energy I wish I had.

Copy-paste prompt 3
Here's my day.
Fixed appointments (with times): [list]
NOW list: [the 3 items]
Other things I'd like to do if there's room: [list]
Energy today: [low / medium / high]
I can reasonably work from [start time] to [end time].

Make a realistic plan with:
- only those 3 must-dos
- a starting time for each, plus a 15–30 minute buffer
- short breaks
- one line for the first action on must-do 1

If it doesn't fit, tell me what moves to tomorrow. Keep it under 150 words. No guilt if the list shrinks.

If the plan still looks packed, say "cut it again." A good plan has empty space on purpose.

When you're stuck, ask for a doorway, not a lecture

Starting is usually the wall, not the work. I don't ask for motivation. I ask for one move I can picture.

Copy-paste prompt 4
I'm stuck on [task]. Don't give me a full plan or a pep talk.

Give me 5 different two-minute ways to start, each one a concrete physical action (open the tab, put the box on the table, write the first sentence). I'll pick one. After I pick, wait — don't pile on the next 12 steps unless I ask.

If even that feels like too much, I use a shorter version: "Give me only one two-minute start for [task]. One sentence."

Redo the afternoon instead of throwing the day out

A lot of my "ruined" days were fine until I tried to keep a morning plan that was no longer true. At lunch I tell it what happened and ask for a smaller afternoon.

Copy-paste prompt 5
Here's what I actually finished: [list].
Here's what's left: [list].
I have from [time] to [time], and my energy is now [low / medium / high].

Re-plan the afternoon. Keep only what fits. Move the rest to tomorrow. Give me one first step for whatever is first. Short, no post-mortem.

This is also useful after a weird morning, a hyperfocus spiral, or a day that got taken over by someone else's urgency.

Safer setup

This is a planning helper, not medical care. It shouldn't diagnose you, manage medication, or replace a clinician, coach, or anyone you already work with. If you use reminders, you should be the one who turns them on, and you can turn off any that start to nag.

Nothing should be sent, posted, bought, or booked without your OK. Don't paste passwords, bank logins, or one-time codes into the chat.

If a prompt ever makes you feel worse, change the wording or stop using it. The point is a lighter day, not a stricter one.
