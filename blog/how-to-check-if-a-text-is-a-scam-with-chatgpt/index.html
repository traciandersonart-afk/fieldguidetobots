Aging parents & scams

How to Use ChatGPT to Check If a Text Is a Scam (for You and Your Parents)

By Traci Anderson · November 26, 2026

My phone gets a "your package couldn't be delivered" text about once a week, and this time of year it's worse. I'm ordering gifts, so half the time I think, wait, is that mine? My ADHD brain wants to tap the link just to make the text go away.

My parents' generation gets the same texts, plus the "Grandma, it's me, I'm in trouble" calls.

A chatbot makes a decent second opinion. You paste the message, and it points out the warning signs: the rush, the odd link, the request for a code or a gift card. It can't see your accounts, though, and it can't prove a message is safe. It's a flashlight, not a lock. You still check the real way, and you decide.

These prompts work in ChatGPT or any similar chatbot. Fill in the brackets, paste, and read what you get.

First rule: don't touch the message

Before you ask anybody, chatbot included: don't tap the link, don't call the number in the message, and don't reply, not even "STOP." Just copy the text (press and hold on a phone, then Copy) or take a screenshot.

Then cover up anything personal before you paste it: your name, address, account numbers, and any code that came with it. The sender's number, the wording, and the link text are what give a scam away. Your details don't help.

Set the helper's job once

Paste this at the start of a new chat, or save it somewhere you can find it.

Copy-paste prompt 1
You're my scam second-opinion helper. I'll paste texts, emails, or call notes that seem off.

For each one:
1) List the warning signs you see, in plain words.
2) Say how suspicious it looks: low, medium, or high. Never tell me it's "definitely safe."
3) Tell me how to check it without using any link, number, or email in the message itself.

Don't ask for my passwords, codes, account numbers, or address. If I start to paste one, remind me to delete it. Keep answers short and calm.

Check one message

Copy-paste prompt 2
I got this message and I'm not sure it's real. I covered up my personal details.

[paste the message, including the sender's number or email address]

Does it look like a scam? List the warning signs. Then tell me the safest way to check whether it's real without tapping anything in the message.

If it says "medium" or "high," you're done. Delete it, or report it first (more on that below). If it says "low," still don't use anything in the message. Open the company's own app, or type their website yourself, and look there.

Find the real way to check

This is where people slip. The text says "call us at" a number, and that number goes straight to the scammer.

Copy-paste prompt 3
This message says it's from [bank / delivery company / store / government office].

Without using anything in the message, how would I check this myself? Tell me where a real phone number is usually found (the back of my card, a past bill, the official app, or the site I type in myself). Don't give me a phone number or link from memory. I'll look it up myself.

I ask it not to give a number from memory on purpose. Chatbots sometimes make up phone numbers that sound right. The back of your card or a past bill is still the gold standard.

The "it's me, I'm in trouble" call

Voice scams are scary because they sound like someone you love. A chatbot can't hear the call, but it can help you get ready before one ever comes.

Copy-paste prompt 4
Help me make a short plan for my family in case someone calls or texts pretending to be a relative in trouble and asks for money, gift cards, or secrecy.

Include: a family code word idea (don't pick it for us; tell me how to choose one), three questions to ask before doing anything, and a reminder that it's always OK to hang up and call the person back on the number we already have.

Write it at a big-print, plain-words level for my [mom / dad / grandparent]. One page.

Print it and put it by the phone. My favorite line for parents is the permission one: you can always hang up. A real grandkid will understand.

Make a cheat sheet for your parents

Copy-paste prompt 5
Turn these rules into a cheat sheet for my [mom / dad], age [age], who is [comfortable / not very comfortable] with a phone:

- Don't tap links in texts you didn't expect
- Never read a code out loud to anyone who calls you
- No real company asks for gift cards
- Hang up, then call the number on your card or bill
- When in doubt, call me at [nickname, not a real number]

Use big, simple words. Five short lines, no scary language. Add one line at the bottom: "It's never rude to hang up."

Write the real phone number on the printout by hand. The chatbot doesn't need it.

If it is a scam

Don't argue with it and don't reply. In the US you can forward spam texts to 7726 (it spells SPAM) and report fraud at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Then block the number and delete the message. If anyone already sent money, a code, or a password, contact the bank or company right away using the number you trust, and change that password from the real site.

Copy-paste prompt 6
I think I just got scammed. Here's what happened, without personal details: [what happened].

Make me a short, calm checklist of what to do in the next hour, in order. Remind me to call my bank or the company using a number I already have, not one from the message.

Safer setup

The helper gives a second opinion. You check, and you decide.

Never paste passwords, one-time codes, card numbers, account numbers, or your Social Security number into a chat. Cover up names and addresses in screenshots. Don't let a chatbot click links, log in for you, or contact anyone on your behalf.

A "low" rating isn't a promise. Always check with the company through its own app, a website you type yourself, or a number from your card or a past bill. This is everyday safety help, not legal or financial advice, and it doesn't replace your bank or the police.
