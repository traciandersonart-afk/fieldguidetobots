Maker pricing

How to Price Handmade Items with AI (and Check the Math Yourself)

By Traci Anderson · November 22, 2026

For years I priced my art by vibes. I'd pick a number that felt polite and hope it worked out. With ADHD, "sit down and do the cost math" sounds simple and then never happens, because it's twelve tiny numbers and every one of them is on a different receipt.

An AI helper is good at the boring part. It can add up materials, spread a pack of supplies across the pieces it makes, and show you what you actually earn per hour. It doesn't know your market or today's fees. You bring the real numbers. It does the adding. You pick the price.

These prompts work in ChatGPT or any similar chatbot. This is planning help, not financial or tax advice. Nothing gets listed, changed, or sent without your OK.

Set the rules first

Paste this before any numbers, so the helper knows its job.

Copy-paste prompt 1
You're my pricing helper for a small handmade shop. Draft only: I set every price, change every listing, and send every quote myself.

Use only the numbers I give you. If something is missing, put "check this" in that spot instead of guessing. Don't look up or assume marketplace fees; I'll give you the current ones.

Show your math line by line so I can check it. Keep it short and plain. No hype, and no promises about sales.

Fees change, and bots love to quote an old number with total confidence. Pull current fees from your marketplace's own fee page.

Figure out what one piece really costs

Pick one product, not the whole shop. Dig out the receipts. Rough is fine.

Copy-paste prompt 2
Help me find the real cost of one [product].

Materials (what I paid for the pack, and about how many pieces it makes):
[Material — paid $___ for ___ — makes about ___ pieces]

Packaging per order: [tissue, mailer, sticker, card, about $___]
Time per piece, including making, photos, listing, and packing: about ___ minutes
The hourly rate I want to pay myself: $___
Monthly shop costs (software, tools, supplies I use up): about $___, and I sell about ___ pieces a month

Make one table: each cost, how you worked it out, and the cost per piece. Total it. Then tell me the cost per piece if I leave my time out, so I can see the difference.

That last line was the eye-opener for me. When you leave your time out, the profit looks great. Put your hours back in and it can shrink a lot. That's not a reason to panic. It's the real number.

Quick check: take one material, divide what you paid by how many pieces it makes, and see if the bot got the same answer. If a $12 pack makes 40 pieces, that's 30 cents a piece. If the bot's number is way off, ask it to redo that line.

Compare a direct price and a wholesale price

If you sell direct and also want shops to carry your work, you need two numbers. A common rule of thumb is cost times two for wholesale, and wholesale times two for retail. It's not a law, but it shows fast whether a shop could ever stock you.

Copy-paste prompt 3
Using my cost per piece from above:

1) Show a wholesale price using cost × 2, and a retail price using wholesale × 2.
2) Show a direct-sale price that leaves me a [__]% profit after fees. My fees are [__]% of the sale price plus $[__] per order. Fees come out of the sale price, so use: price = (cost + per-order fee) ÷ (1 − fee % − profit %).
3) For each price, show what I keep and what that works out to per hour.

End with two sentences on the trade-off. Don't tell me which price to pick.

The formula is in the prompt on purpose. Some bots just add the fee percentage on top, which comes out short, because the fee is charged on the higher price too.

Find the pieces you're underpricing

Once you've done one product, do the rest in a batch. That's how you spot the ones barely covering costs.

Copy-paste prompt 4
Here are my products, with the price I charge now and my best guess at cost per piece and minutes per piece:
[Product — current price $___ — cost about $___ — about ___ minutes]

Make a table: product, current price, cost, what I keep, and what I earn per hour at my current price. Sort it from lowest hourly to highest.

Flag the bottom three. For each, list two or three questions I should think about (raise the price? make it faster? bundle it? retire it?). Questions only, not decisions.

You might keep an underpriced item because it brings people in. Fine. Now it's a choice, not an accident.

Write the price-change note, then decide

If you're raising prices, a short, calm note helps.

Copy-paste prompt 5
Draft a short, friendly note telling my [wholesale shops / regular customers] that prices on [product] go from $___ to $___ starting [date].

Keep it under 80 words. One honest sentence about why (materials and time). No apologizing three times, no fake urgency, no "limited time."

Give me two versions: one for email and one for a shop announcement. I'll edit and send it myself.

Read it out loud. If it doesn't sound like you, ask for a plainer version. Then you decide whether to send it.

A five-minute price check every few months

Costs creep, and you get faster. I set a reminder on my own calendar and run this.

Copy-paste prompt 6
Quarterly price check. Here's what changed since last time:
[Material or packaging cost changes, fee changes, minutes per piece now]

1) Update my cost per piece for each product.
2) Show my hourly at today's prices, next to last time.
3) Flag anything where my hourly dropped.

Don't change any listings or prices. Just show me the table.

Safer setup

Don't paste passwords, bank logins, card numbers, or customer names and addresses into a chat. Product names and rounded numbers are all the helper needs.

The helper adds and drafts. You set the price, edit the listing, and send any notes to customers or shops. Nothing gets listed, changed, sent, or paid without your OK. Check one line of math yourself before you trust the whole table.

This is planning help, not financial, tax, or legal advice, and it isn't a promise about sales.
