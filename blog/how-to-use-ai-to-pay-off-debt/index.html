Debt payoff planning

How to Use AI to Make a Debt Payoff Plan (Without Giving It Your Accounts)

By Traci Anderson · November 21, 2026

Debt is loud in my head and quiet on paper, mostly because I avoid the paper. When I finally sit down, I don't need a lecture. I need one list, one plan I can read in a minute, and somebody to do the math without sighing at me.

An AI helper is good at that part. It can sort a messy list, compare payoff methods, and draft a monthly check-in. It should never get your bank login, and it should never press pay.

These prompts work in ChatGPT or any similar chatbot. This is planning help, not financial advice. Nothing gets paid, moved, or signed without your OK, and the numbers on your real statements always win.

Start with rules, not numbers

Paste this first, so the helper knows its job before it sees anything about your money.

Copy-paste prompt 1
You're a planning helper for my debt payoff. Draft only: I will pay, call, move money, and decide everything myself.

Never ask for passwords, full card or account numbers, Social Security numbers, or one-time codes. If I paste one by mistake, tell me to delete it and don't repeat it.

Don't recommend loans, balance transfers, or products. If something sounds like a question for a credit counselor or my lender, help me write the question instead of answering it as fact.

Keep it short and calm. No pep talk. Flag anything you assumed.

Chatbots sound confident by default. I want the guesses labeled.

Make one honest list

You don't need exact numbers to start. Use nicknames ("Card A," "car loan," "the store card") and round the balances. Pull the APR and minimum payment off your latest statement or account page when you can.

Copy-paste prompt 2
Here are my debts, using nicknames:
[Nickname — balance about $___ — APR ___% — minimum $___ — due day ___ — any promo rate and when it ends]

Put them in one table: nickname, balance, APR, minimum, due day, and a "check this" note for anything I left blank. Add up the total balance and the total of the minimums.

Then ask me one question: how much can I put toward all of these each month, in total? Don't guess that number for me.

A blank isn't a failure. It's a sticky note for later.

Compare snowball and avalanche, and make it show its work

The two common methods are easy to explain. Snowball pays the smallest balance first, for early wins. Avalanche pays the highest interest rate first, which usually costs less overall. Neither one is the "right" answer for everybody. The best one is the one you'll actually stick with.

Copy-paste prompt 3
Using the table above and a total of $[amount] a month toward debt:

1) Show a snowball plan (smallest balance first) and an avalanche plan (highest APR first).
2) For each: the payoff order, roughly when each debt is cleared, and the estimated total interest.
3) Assume every minimum gets paid, freed-up payments roll to the next debt, and no new charges. List any other assumptions.
4) If you can run the math with code or a spreadsheet, do that instead of writing the numbers out.
5) Show the first two months for one debt step by step, so I can check it.

End with two sentences in plain English on the trade-off. Don't tell me which one I "should" pick.

Then check month one yourself. Divide the APR by 12 and multiply by the balance. For example, a 24% APR is about 2% a month, so a $1,000 balance would add about $20 in interest that month. If the bot's number is wildly different, ask it to redo the table. Your card company figures interest its own way, so expect small differences.

Plan for the tight month before it happens

My plans used to fall apart the first rough month. Now I ask for the backup plan up front.

Copy-paste prompt 4
Using my plan, write a "tight month" version.

If I can only pay the minimums this month, what changes, and what stays the same? If I can't cover a minimum, list the questions I should ask my lender or a nonprofit credit counselor, in plain words.

Don't suggest new credit. Keep it to one short list I can find again.

If you can't cover your minimums for more than a month, that's a call for a real person. A nonprofit credit counselor or your lender can tell you what's actually available. The bot can help you write down what to ask.

Draft the phone call, then make it yourself

Calling a card company is a job I put off for weeks. A script helps.

Copy-paste prompt 5
Help me write a short, polite phone script for calling [nickname]'s customer service. I want to ask about [a lower rate / a hardship program / moving my due date / why a fee was charged].

Give me: a two-sentence opening, three questions, a spot to write their answers, and a closing line asking them to note the call on my account.

Don't promise them anything on my behalf. I'll make the call myself.

You dial. You decide. If they offer something, ask them to send it in writing before you agree.

A ten-minute monthly check-in

The plan only works if you look at it. I keep this one short on purpose.

Copy-paste prompt 6
Monthly check-in for my payoff plan. Here's where things stand now:
[Nickname — new balance — anything that changed: rate, minimum, promo ending]

1) Update the table.
2) Tell me which debt is the current target and what to pay on it, based on the plan I picked.
3) Show what changed since last month in one line each.
4) Note any promo rate ending in the next 90 days.

Don't schedule or send anything. I'll make every payment myself.

Put the check-in on your own calendar, maybe the day after payday. Then make your payments where you always do.

Safer setup

Never paste full card numbers, bank logins, routing numbers, Social Security numbers, or one-time codes into a chat. Nicknames and rounded balances are enough to plan.

The helper drafts and does the math. You pay, call, transfer, and sign. Nothing sends, pays, or moves without your OK. Check every number against your statements before you act on it.

This is planning help, not financial advice, and it isn't a promise about how fast you'll be debt-free.
